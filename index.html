01 — The Context

What is Living Heritage, and why does it matter?

Intangible Cultural Heritage (ICH) is the practices, representations, expressions, knowledge and skills a community recognises as its own. It has no walls and no foundations: it resides in minds, voices and hands — transmitted between generations and continuously recreated in response to environment and history. In 2003, UNESCO adopted the Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage, recognising it as a cornerstone of cultural diversity and human creativity.

The Five Domains

Oral Traditions & Expressions

Folktales, poetry, epics, proverbs and the languages that carry them — the primary vehicle of cultural transmission.

Performing Arts

Traditional music, dance and theatre that encode narrative, cosmology and emotion in movement and sound.

Social Practices & Rituals

Rites of passage, festive events and ceremonies that structure daily life and mark the community calendar.

Knowledge of Nature

Traditional ecological wisdom, healing practices, agricultural calendars and community cosmologies.

Traditional Craftsmanship

Not the object, but the skill — material selection, gesture, timing and the tacit knowledge behind making.

A Sense of Identity

Together these domains give communities continuity and belonging — and give humanity its diversity of ways to be human.

Why it is so fragile

Because ICH is carried orally and through practice, it has no backup. Rapid urbanisation, migration, market pressure and generational shift can sever a chain of transmission in a single lifetime.

When a master artisan dies without an apprentice, the heritage does not decay slowly — it simply stops existing.

Documentation is the first line of defence against cultural extinction.

02 — The Method

Inventorying is an act of recognition

An inventory is not a list. Under Articles 11 and 12 of the 2003 Convention, every State Party must identify and define the ICH on its territory with the participation of communities. That obligation turns cataloguing into a safeguarding measure in its own right.

Visibility & Recognition

Brings overlooked practices into official awareness and gives communities formal acknowledgement of their heritage.

Policy & Legal Protection

Supplies the evidential base for safeguarding policy, budget lines, funding programmes and legal frameworks.

Living Transmission

Documented knowledge can reach younger generations even where direct apprenticeship has been interrupted.

Global Dialogue

Opens local heritage to scholarship, exchange and multinational cooperation on shared elements.

How a community-based inventory is built

  1. 1

    Community Identification & Consent

    Communities self-identify their living practices and give free, prior and informed consent (FPIC) before anything is recorded.

  2. 2

    Field Documentation

    Community researchers, ethnographers and archivists gather audio, video, photography and written testimony on location.

  3. 3

    Classification & Viability Assessment

    Elements are categorised by domain, region and viability status using standardised UNESCO frameworks and metadata schemas.

  4. 4

    Publication & Continuous Update

    The inventory is made accessible and reviewed on a cycle — because living heritage keeps changing with its community.

Where ICH Radar fits

Communities already document themselves — just not in registries.

They write blogs, post on regional forums, publish village websites and upload festival footage. That evidence is real, rich and scattered across languages and platforms — and almost entirely invisible to formal inventories.

ICH Radar acts as an autonomous digital archivist — recovering the informal digital footprints communities leave behind and structuring them into formal, interoperable intelligence that supports community-based inventorying.

03 — The Risk Dimension

Disaster Risk Reduction & Living Heritage

Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) is the systematic effort to analyse and reduce the causes of disaster. Intangible heritage sits on both sides of that equation at once: it is among the most fragile casualties of catastrophe, and among the most underused tools for surviving one.

ICH as disaster victim

When communities are displaced, knowledge holders scattered and gathering spaces destroyed, oral traditions, rituals and craft skills can vanish within one generation. Unlike a damaged building, they cannot be rebuilt from rubble.

ICH as disaster resource

Traditional knowledge is also a defence: indigenous early-warning signs, ecological knowledge of flood and tide patterns, vernacular seismic architecture and communal mutual-aid structures have saved communities for centuries.

Global Framework

The Sendai Framework for DRR, 2015–2030

Sendai explicitly recognises local and indigenous knowledge in risk assessment and community resilience. That gives ICH safeguarding a direct mandate inside the global DRR agenda — making inventorying not only a cultural act, but a disaster preparedness strategy.

When the worst happens, the inventory becomes a lifeline

Without a pre-existing record there is no baseline — no way to know what was there, and therefore no way to prove what was lost. An inventory turns an invisible loss into an identifiable, fundable, actionable gap.

Establishing what was lost

Post-disaster assessors can only measure damage against a baseline: which practices, bearers, materials and sites were affected.

Anchoring cultural recovery

Documented ICH gives displaced communities a reference for rebuilding identity when living transmission has been broken.

Unlocking emergency funding

International assistance and emergency requests under the Operational Directives require documented evidence. The inventory is that evidence.

04 — Evidence

Two cases, one lesson

Heritage is not usually lost in a single dramatic moment. It is lost because nobody wrote it down in time — whether the cause is a wave, or simply the passing of centuries.

Case A · 2004 Indian Ocean Tsunami

Knowledge lineages that ended in a morning

The tsunami devastated coastal Aceh — destroying not only lives and infrastructure but entire knowledge lineages: fishermen who read seasonal currents, healers who held medicinal plant knowledge, oral historians who carried community genealogies. Because no systematic ICH inventory existed for many of these communities, much of that knowledge is simply gone, with no record that it ever existed.

Case B · 15th century · Majapahit–Ming era

The Jong of Java — a heritage waiting to be claimed

During the height of Majapahit maritime power and the voyages of Zheng He (1405–1433), Chinese imperial records — most notably Ma Huan's Yingya Shenglan (瀛涯勝覧, 1433) — describe enormous Javanese vessels called jong.

These accounts describe multi-masted ships carrying hundreds of passengers and vast cargo across the Indian Ocean toward Arabia, India and East Africa. Some maritime historians read them as evidence that the Javanese jong rivalled — and by some estimates exceeded — the Chinese treasure ships in scale.

The construction technique was distinctive: hulls built with a dowel-and-lashing method rather than nails — knowledge specialised enough to constitute a maritime ICH domain of its own.

"The local people use these large ships … they are built without nails, the planks being bound together with rattan cords and caulked with coconut fibre."

— Paraphrased from Ma Huan, Yingya Shenglan, c. 1433

The gap in national awareness

Although these accounts survive in foreign imperial archives, this chapter of Javanese maritime heritage has not been widely integrated into curricula, museums or public consciousness — and the knowledge of how these vessels were designed and built has never been systematically inventoried at home.

Heritage in foreign hands

The primary records sit in another nation's archives — the heritage is better documented abroad than at home.

No living transmission

Dowel construction, timber selection and navigational practice trace to no identified living practitioner community.

A call to document

A direct argument for proactive inventorying — before oral memory and material traces disappear entirely.

"A civilisation that once built the world's greatest ships sailed into history without leaving its own written record."

The jong is not a closed chapter — it is an open invitation to document, reclaim and celebrate a heritage still waiting to be fully known.

06 — The Rulebook

Decoding the Operational Directives

The Operational Directives translate the 2003 Convention into practice. Move through the essential chapters below.

Chapter I

Criteria for Inscription

Whether applying to the Representative List (R.1–R.5) for visibility, or the Urgent Safeguarding List (U.1–U.6) for elements in grave danger, strict criteria apply. A third mechanism — Good Safeguarding Practices — recognises programmes that best reflect the Convention's principles.

  • Must constitute ICH as defined in Article 2.
  • Requires an elaborated safeguarding plan.
  • Requires Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC).
  • One file per State Party, per list, per cycle.

Chapter II

International Assistance & the ICH Fund

The ICH Fund provides financial assistance to developing States Parties for safeguarding, inventorying and capacity building. Requests use Form ICH-04. Preparatory assistance has a standing annual deadline of 31 March.

  • Preparatory: support to draft nominations (ICH-04).
  • Safeguarding: funds specific safeguarding projects.
  • Emergency: fast-tracked for disaster or armed conflict.

Chapter III

Community & NGO Participation

Communities, groups and individuals (CGIs) must be actively involved in identifying, defining and inventorying their heritage. States are encouraged to create consultative bodies and to facilitate NGO accreditation.

  • NGOs with proven competence may be formally accredited.
  • Accredited NGOs advise the Intergovernmental Committee.
  • FPIC is required at every stage, not only at the start.
  • Bearers are the primary actors, not passive subjects.

Chapter IV

Raising Awareness

Awareness-raising must fully respect customary practice, especially around secret and sacred elements. Media, tourism and digital platforms are powerful channels — and require ethical oversight, examined further in the Ethics section below.

  • Communities must be the primary beneficiaries of visibility.
  • Respect secrecy and sacredness where customary law applies.
  • Promote ICH as a resource for dialogue, not as spectacle.

Chapter V

Updated 10.GA 2024

Periodic Reporting

Since the 2018 reform, periodic reporting follows a regional cycle rather than one global deadline. States report against 26 core indicators across 8 thematic areas. Asia & the Pacific — including Indonesia — reported in the 2025 cycle (deadline 15 December 2024).

  • ICH-10: general implementation report (~every 6 years).
  • ICH-11: status of Urgent Safeguarding List elements (4 years).
  • ICH-12: report on Representative List elements.

Overall Results Framework

Since 2018

26 Core Indicators

The ORF is the measurement backbone of the Convention. All periodic reports are structured around 26 core indicators and 86 assessment factors across 8 thematic areas, generating a compliance baseline for the next cycle.

  • Policies & legal measures
  • Inventorying & research
  • Transmission & education
  • Community participation & FPIC
  • Sustainable development linkages
  • International cooperation & capacity building

Chapter VI

Sustainable Development

ICH is a recognised driver of the 2030 Agenda. States Parties must integrate safeguarding into development planning while balancing social, economic and environmental dimensions — including disaster risk reduction.

  • Social: food security, health, education, gender equality.
  • Environment: biodiversity, climate resilience, DRR.
  • Peace: cohesion, inclusive governance, dispute resolution.

Emergency Situations

OD §22

ICH in Crisis & Disaster

The Directives include a dedicated mechanism for emergency safeguarding. Where ICH faces imminent threat from natural disaster, armed conflict or public health emergency, States may request expedited assistance outside the regular cycle.

  • Fast-tracked requests — no fixed deadline.
  • Applies to elements facing imminent disappearance.
  • A pre-existing inventory is critical to activating it.
  • Undocumented heritage cannot be claimed or recovered.

07 — Ethical Boundaries

The fine line: safeguarding or selling?

Trade and tourism can sustain practitioners and strengthen local economies. Chapter IV of the Operational Directives also warns, explicitly, against the practices that hollow living heritage out from the inside.

Ethical commercialisation

Well-governed trade in cultural goods and services can raise living standards and reinforce social cohesion.

  • Communities and practitioners are the primary beneficiaries of income generated.
  • Customary rules governing access to secret and sacred aspects are fully respected.
  • Scale is managed so that transmission of skills to the next generation continues.
  • Practitioners retain authorship, pricing power and the right to say no.

Commercial misappropriation

Extracting heritage for short-term gain distorts its meaning and severs it from the community that gives it life.

  • Decontextualisation: sacred rites staged purely as tourist entertainment.
  • Misappropriation: outside entities profiting from traditional knowledge without consent or benefit-sharing.
  • Over-commercialisation: mass production that destroys the craftsmanship process it imitates.
  • Freezing: locking a practice into a "marketable" form so it can no longer evolve.

08 — The Ecosystem

Who actually makes safeguarding work?

At the centre are the bearers and communities who keep practices alive. Around them sit two habitually overlooked catalysts: the media that shapes public consciousness, and the consultative bodies that bridge state bureaucracy and grassroots reality.

Part I — Media

"Heritage the public cannot see, hear or feel is heritage the public will not protect."

— The visibility problem in ICH safeguarding

The Operational Directives recognise media as a channel for awareness-raising — yet journalists covering ICH are rare, coverage is shallow, and heritage usually makes headlines only once it is already at the edge of extinction. Media is never a neutral observer of heritage; it is a participant in whether heritage survives.

Visibility & legitimacy

Respectful coverage confers social legitimacy: communities feel pride rather than embarrassment, and the young see the practice as worth learning.

Early-warning system

Investigative and documentary work surfaces threats — land conversion, the death of a final bearer — faster than any policy cycle can react.

Documentation in itself

Local-language radio, oral-history podcasts and performance archives are acts of inventorying. A good media archive is an ICH record.

Political will & funding

Budgets follow attention. One documentary reaching millions can move more funding than a dozen policy briefs read by a handful of officials.

Cross-cultural dialogue

International coverage builds the public awareness that multinational safeguarding cooperation depends on as a precondition.

The risk: spectacle without substance

Careless media harms — reducing sacred practice to entertainment, misrepresenting communities, inviting appropriation. Ethical coverage requires consent and cultural literacy.

Part II — Consultative Group

The national bridge between Convention and community

The 2003 Convention is an international instrument — but heritage lives locally. A national Consultative Group (also called an Expert Committee or National ICH Council) is the mechanism that converts UN-level obligations into village-level action. Without it, the Convention remains ink on paper.

What is a Consultative Group?

A multi-stakeholder advisory body at national or sub-national level, made up of cultural officials, academic researchers, NGO representatives, community practitioners and independent experts. It advises government on identification, inventorying, nomination and safeguarding priorities.

The Republic of Korea offers a mature model: its Intangible Cultural Heritage Committee, under the Korea Heritage Service, is a formalised, unified body integrating scholars, master practitioners and policymakers to steer national safeguarding and UNESCO nominations.

OD Chapter III · Convention Arts. 11 & 12

Who typically sits on it?

Government cultural bodies

Ministries and regional agencies for culture, education and tourism.

Researchers & ethnographers

Technical expertise in documentation, classification and viability assessment.

Community bearers & practitioners

The most important voice — and the one most frequently missing from the table.

Accredited NGOs & civil society

Field-competent organisations bridging institutional and community worlds.

What does it actually do?

Function 01
Validates the national inventory

Reviews and endorses which elements qualify for listing under Articles 11–12, applying agreed criteria and domain classification.

Function 02
Prioritises nomination candidates

Judges which inventoried elements are ready for the Representative List, Urgent Safeguarding List or Good Practices register.

Function 03
Ensures genuine FPIC

Verifies that consent is real and informed — not a signature collected at the end of the process.

Function 04
Feeds the periodic reports

Supplies inventory data, safeguarding assessments and community feedback for Forms ICH-10, -11 and -12.

Function 05
Advises on DRR & emergencies

In disaster contexts, rapidly assesses affected heritage, bearers in need, and which emergency assistance to request.

Function 06
Bridges policy and practice

Translates Convention obligations into national legislation, regional programmes and local cultural policy.

Without a Consultative Group

Inventory decisions are taken by administrators without field knowledge, producing incomplete or inaccurate listings.

Nominations are drafted without genuine community participation — risking rejection or post-inscription conflict.

Periodic reports carry generic data, masking which elements are actually at risk.

After a disaster there is no coordinated mechanism to establish what was lost or what assistance to request.

Better together

A Consultative Group without media reach works invisibly. Media without a Consultative Group amplifies without verifying. Together they form a reinforcing loop: documented heritage becomes visible, visible heritage gains political protection, and protected heritage survives.

10 — Common Questions

Frequently asked

What is Intangible Cultural Heritage (ICH)?

The practices, representations, expressions, knowledge and skills that communities recognise as part of their cultural heritage. Unlike monuments, it lives in people — transmitted between generations and constantly recreated in response to environment and history.

What are the five domains of ICH?

Oral traditions and expressions; performing arts; social practices, rituals and festive events; knowledge and practices concerning nature and the universe; and traditional craftsmanship.

What is the UNESCO 2003 Convention?

The Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage — the international framework obliging States Parties to identify, inventory and safeguard living heritage with the participation of the communities that bear it.

What is community-based inventorying?

A method in which communities themselves identify, define and document their heritage under free, prior and informed consent — rather than being catalogued from the outside by experts alone.

How does ICH relate to disaster risk reduction?

ICH is both a casualty of disasters and a resource for resilience. The Sendai Framework 2015–2030 recognises local and indigenous knowledge in risk assessment, and a pre-existing inventory is essential to activate emergency assistance and guide cultural recovery.

Is ICH Radar an official UNESCO platform?

No. ICH Radar is an independent educational project that curates publicly available UNESCO capacity-building materials for learning and reference. All original resources remain the property of UNESCO and their respective rights holders.

Representative List or Urgent Safeguarding List?

The Representative List (criteria R.1–R.5) exists to raise visibility and encourage dialogue. The Urgent Safeguarding List (U.1–U.6) is for elements whose viability is at severe risk and requires a robust, funded safeguarding plan.

Can living heritage be commercialised ethically?

Yes — when communities are the primary beneficiaries, customary rules on secret and sacred aspects are respected, and transmission of skills is sustained. Decontextualisation, misappropriation and over-commercialisation are explicitly warned against in Chapter IV of the Operational Directives.
